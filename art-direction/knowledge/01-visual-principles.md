# Visual Principles

Status: universal knowledge.

This file defines practical relationships between foundational visual-design
principles. It is not a brand specification.

## Hierarchy

Hierarchy is the intended order in which information is noticed and
understood.

Hierarchy may be created through:

- scale;
- weight;
- position;
- contrast;
- spacing;
- grouping;
- isolation;
- motion;
- repetition and interruption.

Contrast is one mechanism for hierarchy. The two terms are not interchangeable:
contrast describes difference, while hierarchy describes ordered importance.

Check:

- Is the primary element found first without explanation?
- Are secondary and tertiary levels distinguishable?
- Does semantic importance match visual emphasis?
- Does the hierarchy survive narrow widths and long content?

## Contrast

Contrast is a meaningful difference between visual properties.

Possible dimensions:

- light and dark;
- large and small;
- dense and open;
- heavy and light;
- static and moving;
- regular and irregular;
- saturated and muted;
- sharp and soft.

Contrast should communicate structure, state, or emphasis. Decorative contrast
without a role creates noise.

## Balance

Balance is the distribution of visual weight across a composition. It does not
require symmetry.

Visual weight may come from:

- size;
- darkness or saturation;
- density;
- detail;
- isolation;
- edge position;
- motion;
- semantic importance.

Check both:

- local balance inside a component;
- global balance across the section or viewport.

## Unity

Unity is the perception that separate elements belong to one coherent system.

Unity is strengthened through:

- shared alignment lines;
- repeated spacing relationships;
- controlled type roles;
- recurring geometry;
- consistent surface logic;
- stable interaction behavior;
- recognizable component signatures.

Unity does not mean uniformity. A system may contain contrast and variation
while preserving a common grammar.

## Rhythm And Repetition

Rhythm is the pattern produced by repeated intervals, dimensions, alignments,
or visual events.

Use repetition to establish expectation. Interrupt repetition only to signal a
meaningful change in hierarchy, state, or narrative.

Inspect:

- vertical spacing sequences;
- repeated card anatomy;
- baseline relationships;
- section alternation;
- repeated control heights;
- media and text cadence.

## Proportion And Scale

Proportion describes relationships between parts. Scale describes size
relative to another object, context, or viewport.

A coherent system uses a limited family of relationships rather than unrelated
large and small values. Optical correction is allowed when mathematical
equality produces visual imbalance.

## Alignment

Alignment creates visible relationships between elements. Strong alignments
reduce the amount of explanation a layout requires.

Prefer:

- shared container edges;
- consistent text starts;
- deliberate baseline relationships;
- component internals aligned to external layout logic.

Avoid accidental near-alignment. Elements should either align or establish an
intentional offset rule.

## Proximity And Grouping

Distance communicates relationship. Related elements should normally be closer
to one another than to adjacent groups.

Use proximity before adding borders, backgrounds, or labels. A surface should
not compensate for unclear spacing relationships.

## Negative Space

Negative space is an active compositional element, not unused area.

It may:

- isolate a focal point;
- separate semantic groups;
- create calm or tension;
- reveal grid structure;
- control reading pace;
- balance dense content.

Negative space must be intentional and relational. Large empty areas without a
role are not automatically sophisticated.

## Tension

Tension is controlled instability or contrast that creates attention.

It may come from:

- asymmetry;
- cropping;
- offsets;
- scale shifts;
- edge proximity;
- interruption of a repeated pattern.

Tension must remain subordinate to usability and content comprehension.

## Coherence Test

A visual direction is coherent when:

- its principles can be observed in multiple component families;
- the same rule explains both small controls and large compositions;
- exceptions are named and limited;
- removing brand assets does not remove all recognisable character;
- the system can generate new work without copying an existing screen.
